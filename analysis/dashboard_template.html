Which promotions actually make money, how much should suppliers fund, and where should next quarter's promotion budget go?

Where each $1 of promotion spend goes

The extra sales nearly pay for themselves: the margin on extra units almost covers the discount on those units. The loss comes from the same discount handed to shoppers who were going to buy anyway.

Gross profit uses assumed department margins (the data has no costs). Promotion spend is discount beyond each product's everyday card price. Supplier funding is not included here; the Keep or fix section shows how much a supplier would need to cover.

Promotion types compared

In-store displays pay back on their own. Mailer-plus-display events drive the biggest lift but carry deep discounts. Price cuts with no mailer or display are the worst value, because more than half the units would have sold anyway.

How deep should the extra discount be?

The profit-maximising extra discount for each department, given the share a supplier funds. At 25% supplier funding or less, the best extra discount is zero in every department. Even at 50%, today's extra discounts are deeper than the best level everywhere.

Do promotions bring extra spend on other items?

The usual defence of a losing promotion is that it brings shoppers in, and they fill a bigger basket. This was tested directly, and it does not hold here.

Do targeted campaigns create extra spend? Not proven.

Campaign households spend far more than everyone else, but they already did before the campaign: the retailer targets its best shoppers. Comparing like with like, and measuring on weeks not used for matching, the extra spend shrinks to an amount this data cannot tell apart from zero.

How this was measured

Data. 84.51° "The Complete Journey": every grocery purchase by 2,469 households over 2017 (1.46M basket lines), with weekly mailer and in-store display placement by product and store, 27 targeted campaigns, and coupons. Promotion economics use the 2,186 products that sold in at least 45 of 53 weeks, covering 59% of sales.

Promotion spend. Each product's everyday card discount is its median discount depth over the year. Only discount beyond that counts as promotion spend. A week is a price-cut promotion if the extra discount is 10 points or more; mailer and display need 50% store coverage.

Lift. A two-way fixed-effects demand model (product, and department × week) estimates the extra units from each promotion type and each point of extra discount, the change in the weeks after, and the sales taken from other items of the same type. Intervals come from 300 draws of the coefficient sampling distribution, clustered by product.

Model check. One in five promoted weeks was held out of the fit and predicted afterwards.

Baseline for held-out promoted weeksBias in total unitsError (WAPE)

Halo. Household preferences fixed from weeks 1–26 are combined with the chain's promotion calendar to give each household's exposure in weeks 27–53 (a shift-share design), with household and week fixed effects and a 4-week-ahead placebo.

Profit. Gross profit uses stated department margins on the everyday price (grocery 25%, produce 40%, meat 28%, deli 40%, bakery 55%, health and general merchandise 30%) because the data has no cost prices. The sliders above show how the answer moves with those assumptions.

Campaigns. Each campaign household is matched to a non-campaign household with similar spend, trips and trend in the matching weeks, then compared on the change in spend between four holdout weeks and the campaign weeks.

Limits. One retailer, one year, a household panel rather than full store sales. Supplier funding, mailing costs and competitor prices are not observed. Effects beyond two weeks (long-run loyalty, price image) are not measured.
